"What's your MBTI, by the way?" - The 16 Magical Alphabets Ruling South Korea

These days in Korea, there is a question asked before age, occupation, or hometown when meeting someone for the first time. That question is, "What's your MBTI?" Just a decade ago, there was an era where people categorized and guessed personalities based on four blood types (A, B, O, AB), but that era is completely over. The 16-letter combination of MBTI has perfectly taken its place. MBTI has now gone beyond a simple internet psychological test to become an essential "digital business card" and social barcode needed to navigate Korean society.

The most noticeable change appears in the dating and interpersonal relationships of the 2030 generation (people in their 20s and 30s). If the go-to questions at blind dates used to be "What are your hobbies?" or "What kind of food do you like?", nowadays, asking "What is your MBTI?" as soon as you sit down has become the guk-rul (universal rule/standard). Writing your MBTI on your dating app profile is the most basic of basics. There are even many people who block types they think won't match with them right from the start, saying things like, "I'm an extreme 'E' (Extrovert), so I don't get along with 'I's (Introverts) who only stay at home," or "Only people with an emotional 'F' (Feeling) tendency, please contact me." Rather than experiencing and judging a person deeply over time, they pre-calculate their compatibility with the other person through four letters.

The trend-sensitive marketing market hasn't missed out on this craze, either. If you go to Seoul's "hot places" (trendy spots) like the streets of Hongdae or Seongsu-dong these days, you can easily find shops utilizing MBTI. For example, at a capsule fortune-telling shop in Hongdae, there are separate machines prepared for each of the 16 MBTI types. People happily put in just 1,000 won to draw a customized fortune slip perfectly tailored to their personality type. At convenience stores, "MBTI beer"—where buying four cans lets you combine the letters—sold like hotcakes, and banks have even launched "MBTI customized savings accounts" that analyze consumption patterns according to a customer's disposition. It has truly become the most powerful keyword for opening consumers' wallets.

However, this craze isn't always consumed just as a fun meme. The place where it's becoming the most controversial is the job market. In recent years, companies have emerged that require applicants to mandatorily write their MBTI on their resumes when hiring part-timers or new employees. A specific cafe sparked a discrimination controversy by posting a job ad stating, "Only active 'E' (Extrovert) types can apply," and at one large corporation's interview, the question "Explain your MBTI's strengths and weaknesses in connection to our company's role" appeared. Regarding the phenomenon where the results of a self-reported psychological test become a hiring standard, experts warn, "It is dangerous to judge an individual's complex capabilities with just a few letters."

Even in everyday conversation, MBTI has completely changed language habits. The most representative catchphrase is undoubtedly, "Are you a T?" It's a scolding remark used when someone tells a sad story hoping for comfort, but the other person doesn't empathize and instead only offers a solution in a too rational and cold manner. Conversely, when going on a trip with friends, you might marvel at a friend who brings an Excel schedule planned down to the minute, saying, "You are a total Power J (Judging/Planning type)!"

So why are Koreans in particular so enthusiastic and overly immersed in MBTI? Many psychologists and sociologists analyze that this shows a cross-section of Korea's unique pali-pali (hurry, hurry) culture and fiercely competitive society. This is because, in a busy and tiring modern society, there is no luxury of time to put in the long effort to figure others out. Therefore, people want to put others into 16 set categories to quickly and efficiently predict them. Also, due to the nature of Korean culture that values a strong sense of belonging, people gain psychological stability from the fact that "there are so many people of the same type as me."

Of course, the diverse and complex personalities of a population of 8 billion cannot be perfectly divided and contained in merely 16 boxes. Just as the "me" of yesterday and the "me" of today are different, humans are beings that constantly change depending on the situation. Blindly trusting MBTI and having prejudices against others is certainly an attitude to be wary of. However, if used as a tool to acknowledge that oneself and others are "different" and to positively understand those differences, MBTI is undoubtedly the most cheerful and interesting medium of communication in Korean society today.
