[How to Turn Your Boss's Nagging into 'Data', and Your Hardships into a 'Story']

When working at a company, there are many times when it's harder because of people than the work itself. You get pressured by your boss from above, and sometimes you have to listen to the complaints of your juniors from below. When you feel depressed, thinking, "Why on earth are they doing this to me?", how can we endure this difficult reality?

The famous scholar Viktor Frankl said, "When we are no longer able to change a situation, we are challenged to change ourselves." If we cannot change the people and situations in reality as we wish, how about changing our 'perspective' (gaze) looking at them? I will introduce two interesting methods that help when you are stressed at work.

First Method: “Could this be material for my autobiography (my life story)?” – Distancing oneself
Successful novels or stories have something in common. A great hardship happens to the main character and a bad villain appears, but eventually, the main character overcomes the crisis and grows.

When I was working at a company, there was a junior colleague who became very successful writing a web novel. Surprisingly, the content of that novel was 'things that happened at the company'. He observed the company and his bosses very closely to set the main character as a CEO. As he kept pondering, "How would a CEO think and act in this situation?", that junior colleague suddenly grew into a brilliant talent who thinks broadly like a CEO.

In psychology, this is called 'self-distancing'. When you are emotionally having a very hard time, just looking at yourself as a third-person observer (from another person's perspective) greatly reduces stress. If you imagine your life as a single novel, you become the 'main character' who overcomes the crisis, rather than a 'victim' suffering. When a boss unfairly stresses you out, if you look at it objectively from a distance in your mind, thinking, "Is this a massive crisis enough to go into my autobiography?", most things just feel like a common episode.

Second Method: “Making the boss's nagging into data”
Recently, an employee at an IT company did something very amazing. Instead of getting hurt by the boss's sharp criticisms, that employee made a brilliant choice. Over the past 6 months, he gathered thousands of criticism (nagging) messages the boss made on messengers and in meetings, and trained an AI (Artificial Intelligence) with them. Then, he made a 'virtual boss robot' that gives feedback in the boss's tone of voice in advance before submitting a proposal.

The AI he made pinpointed exactly what the boss disliked: (1) Writing vaguely without numbers, (2) Copying and pasting the results made by AI as they are without one's own thoughts, (3) Passively doing only what was ordered.

This employee didn't think of the boss's fierce scolding as an 'attack towards me'. Instead, he viewed it as 'good data' that will grow himself and the organization. Instead of falling into bad feelings, he learned the leader's way of thinking entirely using the tool called AI. When the boss's nagging became a predictable 'data system', the employee's hurt feelings disappeared like melting snow.

Did you write a page of your autobiography today?
The stress encountered in the field, a boss's nagging, and conflict with colleagues are unavoidable realities. However, the attitude of dealing with them is entirely our choice. Don't be consumed by feelings of unfairness and miss the 'opportunity for growth' hidden inside it.

Look at your leaders and colleagues again with this thought. They are not villains tormenting you, but multi-dimensional characters in your novel and precious data training you.

If your heart was shaken by someone's thorny words today, quietly say this to yourself inside:
"Good, this is pretty useful story material."
And perhaps a few years later, looking much more relaxed, you will remember that moment and say this:
"That's right, that scene completely changed the story of my life."
